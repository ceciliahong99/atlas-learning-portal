What Is Continuum?

Continuum is a mission-design tool: you describe a satellite mission on a computer, and it simulates how that mission would actually behave in space — before anyone spends a dollar building hardware.

Analogy: think of Continuum like architecture software for satellites. An architect doesn't pour concrete to test if a building design works — they model it first. Continuum lets space engineers do the same thing: model an orbit, a spacecraft, and a target, then see whether the design actually accomplishes the mission's goal.

Why simulate instead of just building?

Spacecraft are expensive and impossible to repair once launched. A single design mistake — an orbit that spends too much time in Earth's shadow, or a battery too small to survive it — can end a mission worth tens of millions of dollars. Simulation lets engineers try thousands of "what if" variations for free, long before committing to hardware.

Before touching orbits or spacecraft, it helps to understand how the software organizes your work — like knowing what a "folder" and a "file" are before you use a computer.

The container hierarchy

🚀

A Project is the top-level workspace — think of it as a folder that groups related work together (e.g. "2027 Earth Observation Study"). Inside a project you can create, rename, or delete any number of Missions. Each mission is a self-contained design: a specific orbit, a specific spacecraft, specific targets. Changing one mission doesn't affect the others, so teams often create several missions to compare design options side-by-side.

Analogy: a Project is like a shared Google Drive folder for a research question. Each Mission inside it is a separate document — a distinct "version" of the design you're exploring.

Creating a new mission: two starting points

Start from scratch — give it a project name and mission name, then build every input yourself (orbit, spacecraft, targets).
Use the Mission Guide — a guided wizard that walks you through the three most important early decisions in order:
1. Orbit — where will the spacecraft fly? (see Module 2)
2. Payload — what instrument does the mission carry? (see Module 3)
3. Vendor — whose hardware/manufacturing options will be considered? (see Module 3)

Module 2 · Orbital Mechanics

What Is an Orbit?

An orbit is not "floating in space" — it's a very fast, continuous fall that keeps missing the ground.

Newton's cannonball thought experiment: the faster the cannonball is fired, the farther it travels before gravity pulls it down. Fire it fast enough, and the ground curves away beneath it just as fast as it falls — so it never lands.

The tug-of-war that makes an orbit

Every orbit is a balance between two things pulling against each other:

⬇️ Gravity — constantly pulling the spacecraft straight down toward the center of the planet.
➡️ Forward velocity — the spacecraft's sideways speed, which keeps trying to carry it off in a straight line.

Neither one wins. Gravity keeps bending the spacecraft's path back toward the planet, while its sideways speed keeps carrying it forward — the result is a closed curve (usually an ellipse) that repeats forever, as long as nothing else interferes (Module 2 covers a few things that do interfere: drag, uneven gravity, etc.).

Key intuition: a satellite is not "escaping gravity" — gravity is exactly what keeps it orbiting instead of flying off into space. Remove gravity entirely and the satellite would travel in a straight line, away from Earth forever.

Two flavors of orbit shape

Circular orbit — constant altitude and constant speed. Simple, common for communication and Earth-observation satellites that want a steady, predictable view.
Elliptical orbit — altitude and speed both vary continuously: fastest and lowest at "perigee" (closest point), slowest and highest at "apogee" (farthest point).
Module 2 · Orbital Mechanics

Central Bodies: What Is the Spacecraft Orbiting?

Before you can define an orbit, you have to say what it's an orbit around.

Continuum lets a mission be built around different central bodies — the object whose gravity dominates the spacecraft's motion. The two most common choices are:

🌍 Earth

The default choice for almost all practical missions — communications, weather, imaging, GPS-like navigation. Earth's gravity, atmosphere, and shape all factor into the simulation.

🌕 Moon

Used for lunar missions — orbiters, landers, or relay satellites that support surface missions. The Moon has no atmosphere and much weaker gravity, so the physics simplify in some ways (no drag) and change in others (very different gravity field).

Why the central body matters: everything downstream — gravity strength, whether atmospheric drag applies, how eclipses happen, even how fast the body rotates beneath the spacecraft — depends on which body you pick. Switching a mission from Earth to the Moon isn't a small tweak; it changes almost every input in the simulation.
PropertyEarthMoon
Atmosphere (drag applies?)Yes, up to ~700 kmNo — essentially none
Relative gravity strength1× (reference)~0.17×
Rotation period24 hours~27.3 days
Typical missionsComms, imaging, navigation, scienceLunar orbiters, relay satellites, landers

Orbital Elements: Describing an Orbit With Numbers

An orbit's exact size, shape, and orientation can be fully described with just six numbers, called the classical (or "Keplerian") orbital elements.

Part 1 — the shape of the ellipse


spacecraft

Earth sits at one focus of the ellipse (not the center). The orbit is closest at perigee and farthest at apogee.

Semi-major axis (a) — half the "long way across" the ellipse. Essentially, the orbit's overall size — bigger a means a bigger, slower, higher orbit.
Eccentricity (e) — how "squashed" the ellipse is, from 0 (perfect circle) to just under 1 (a long, thin ellipse).

Part 2 — the orbit's orientation in space

Three more numbers pin down how the orbital plane is tilted and rotated in 3D space around Earth.

All six, at a glance

#ElementPlain-language meaning
1Semi-major axis (a)How big the orbit is overall
2Eccentricity (e)How stretched/oval the orbit is
3Inclination (i)How tilted the orbit is relative to the equator
4Right ascension of ascending node (Ω / RAAN)Which way the tilted plane is "facing" in space
5Argument of perigee (ω)Where the closest point sits within the tilted plane
6True anomaly (ν)Where the spacecraft is right now along its path
Why six numbers? A satellite's position and velocity in 3D space actually take six numbers to describe fully (three for position, three for velocity). The orbital elements are just a more intuitive repackaging of those same six numbers — describing the shape and orientation of the path rather than a single instantaneous position and velocity.
Module 2 · Orbital Mechanics

Trajectories & Ground Tracks

Two different ways to visualize where a spacecraft actually goes.

Trajectory — the spacecraft's full 3D path through space over time (its position at every moment, in three dimensions).
Ground track — project that same path straight down onto the Earth's surface, and trace the line it draws — the "shadow" of the orbit on a flat map.

Example ground track for an inclined orbit: a repeating wave pattern as the satellite crosses back and forth over the equator while Earth rotates beneath it.

Because Earth keeps spinning underneath the spacecraft, even a perfectly repeating orbit traces a different line across the map on each pass — that's why ground tracks look like a series of parallel "S" curves marching sideways across a world map instead of retracing the exact same line every time.

Why it matters for mission design: if a mission's goal is to photograph a specific city every day, the ground track — not just the orbit's shape — determines whether the satellite will actually pass over that city, and how often.

Reading a trajectory plot

Altitude over time for an elliptical orbit — rising to apogee, falling to perigee, repeating every orbital period.

Module 2 · Orbital Mechanics

Gravity Models: How Precisely Should We Model "Down"?

Earth isn't a perfect sphere with perfectly even gravity — and how much of that mess you model changes how accurate (and how slow) a simulation is.

Every gravity model is a trade-off between simplicity/speed and accuracy. Continuum offers three increasingly detailed options:

Point Mass

Treats Earth as a perfect sphere with gravity pulling evenly from a single point at the center. Fast, simple, good enough for rough estimates.

J2

Adds Earth's biggest real imperfection: it bulges slightly at the equator (like a very slightly squashed ball) due to its own spin. This one correction captures most of the real-world effect.

Spherical Harmonics

Models the full "lumpy" gravity field — mountains, ocean trenches, and dense/light patches of crust all pull slightly differently. Most accurate, most computationally expensive.

Real-world evidence: satellite missions like NASA/German GRACE have mapped Earth's actual gravity field in detail, producing "lumpy potato" globe visualizations where bulges show regions of slightly stronger gravity (extra mass) and dips show slightly weaker gravity. That real, measured unevenness is exactly what the spherical harmonics model captures mathematically.

Which one should a mission use?

ModelBest forTrade-off
Point MassEarly design, quick "does this orbit roughly make sense" checksFast but noticeably inaccurate over long durations
J2Most everyday mission planningCaptures ~90% of the real effect for a small computational cost
Spherical HarmonicsFinal, high-precision verification before launchMost realistic, but slowest to compute

Atmospheric Drag

Even hundreds of kilometers up, there's just enough leftover atmosphere to slowly drag a satellite out of the sky.

Air molecules don't stop abruptly at some altitude — they simply get rarer and rarer with height. In "Low Earth Orbit" (roughly 200–700 km up), there's still just enough of them that a spacecraft moving at ~7.8 km/s constantly bumps into a few, losing a tiny bit of energy every second.

Simplified example: altitude slowly decaying over time due to drag. Lower starting altitude → thicker air → faster decay.

📉 Effect: drag steadily removes energy from the orbit, causing altitude to shrink over time — left unchecked, this is how old satellites and space debris eventually burn up in the atmosphere.
⛽ Countermeasure: many low-orbiting satellites carry thrusters and periodically "boost" themselves back up to counteract drag — this uses fuel, so drag directly limits a mission's lifetime.
Why altitude matters so much: drag force roughly follows air density, which drops off exponentially with altitude. A satellite at 300 km can decay and re-enter within weeks to months; one at 700 km might stay up for decades — a huge design consideration for how much fuel to carry, or whether you need fuel at all.

Eclipses: When the Satellite Loses the Sun

Once per orbit (for many satellites), Earth itself blocks the sunlight — and with it, solar power.

As the satellite orbits, part of its path passes through Earth's shadow — cutting off sunlight and solar power for that portion of the orbit.

For a satellite in low Earth orbit, this shadow period typically lasts something like 30–40 minutes out of a ~90-minute orbit — meaning the spacecraft can spend roughly a third of every orbit with zero solar power.

🔋 Why it matters for design: spacecraft need batteries sized to survive the longest eclipse in their orbit while still running critical systems (see Module 3: Power Budget).
🌡️ Secondary effect: temperature swings between sunlight and eclipse also stress hardware — engineers must design for both extremes, not just the average.
Orbit choice changes eclipse behavior: some special orbits (like certain "sun-synchronous" orbits) are deliberately chosen to minimize or regularize eclipse time, trading orbital flexibility for more predictable power availability.
Module 2 · Orbital Mechanics

Clouds: Why Weather Shows Up in Orbit Simulations

Gravity and drag affect where a satellite goes — clouds affect whether it can actually see anything once it gets there.

Unlike gravity or drag, clouds don't change a spacecraft's physical path at all. Instead, they're modeled because many missions exist specifically to look at the ground — Earth-imaging, agriculture monitoring, disaster response — and clouds simply block that view, just like they would block a photo taken from an airplane.

📷 Optical payloads (ordinary cameras) are completely blocked by cloud cover — no image of the ground is possible on a cloudy pass.
📡 Radar payloads can often see straight through clouds, since radio waves pass through water vapor far better than visible light does.

By including average cloud-cover statistics for a target region, Continuum can help estimate how often a mission will actually get a usable, cloud-free look at its target — which matters enormously for missions with a strict revisit-time requirement (e.g. "photograph this field once a week").

Why it matters: a mission can have a perfect orbit and a perfect camera, and still fail to deliver useful data if the target region is cloudy 80% of the time. Modeling clouds turns "can the satellite physically see the target?" into a realistic probability instead of an optimistic assumption.
Module 3 · Spacecraft & Payload

Anatomy of a Spacecraft

Every satellite, no matter its mission, splits into two fundamental parts: the bus and the payload.

The bus: the spacecraft's "body"

The bus is everything that keeps the spacecraft alive and functioning, regardless of its mission — the same basic bus design can carry a camera, a radio, or a science instrument. It typically includes:

🏗️ Structure — the physical frame holding everything together and surviving launch.
🔋 Power system — solar arrays + batteries (see Power Budget).
🧠 Avionics/computer — onboard "brain" running software and handling commands.
📡 Communications — antennas for talking to ground stations (see Module 4).
🚀 Propulsion (optional) — thrusters for orbit adjustments, like counteracting drag.
🎯 Attitude control — keeps the spacecraft correctly oriented (e.g. solar panels facing the sun, instrument facing the target).

The payload: the reason the mission exists

The payload is the mission-specific hardware — the actual instrument or equipment that accomplishes the mission's goal. Everything else on the spacecraft exists to support the payload. The next page covers common payload types and how a mission chooses between different manufacturers ("vendors") to build one.

Analogy: think of the bus like a delivery truck, and the payload like the cargo. The truck (bus) provides power, navigation, and structure — but the entire trip only matters because of what's in the back.
Module 3 · Spacecraft & Payload

Payload Types & Vendors

What the satellite actually does, and who builds it.

Common payload categories

📷 Optical imaging — cameras that photograph the Earth's surface (agriculture, mapping, disaster monitoring). Blocked by clouds (see Module 2).
📡 Radar (SAR) — active sensors that bounce radio waves off the ground; work day or night and through clouds.
📶 Communications — relays that receive and re-transmit signals (TV, internet, phone service) between ground locations.
🧪 Science instruments — specialized sensors for research (magnetometers, spectrometers, particle detectors, etc.).
Why payload choice drives everything else: the payload's power draw, mass, pointing accuracy needs, and data output all cascade into the mass budget, power budget, and requirements covered next — the payload is usually the starting point the rest of the spacecraft is designed around.

Vendors: who builds it?

A vendor is the company or manufacturer supplying a piece of hardware — a specific camera model, a specific propulsion system, or an entire spacecraft bus. Real-world vendors range from large aerospace primes to specialized small-satellite component suppliers.

Comparing vendors early in mission design matters because different vendors offer different trade-offs:

Trade-offWhat it affects
CostTotal mission budget
Mass & power drawFeeds directly into the mass and power budgets
Heritage / flight historyRisk — a component "flown before" is generally lower-risk than a brand-new design
Lead timeHow long until the hardware is ready, affecting the mission schedule

By letting a mission swap between vendor options, Continuum makes it possible to compare "what if we used Vendor A's camera vs. Vendor B's camera" without redoing the entire mission design from scratch.

Requirements: Defining "Success" Before You Build Anything

A requirement is a measurable rule the finished mission must satisfy — the yardstick every design decision gets checked against.

Before designing hardware, mission teams write down specific, testable statements about what the mission must accomplish. Good requirements are measurable — not "take good pictures," but something like "achieve ground resolution of 5 meters or better."

✅ Good requirement: "The spacecraft shall maintain communication with a ground station at least once every 12 hours." — specific, testable, unambiguous.
❌ Weak requirement: "The spacecraft should communicate frequently." — vague, can't be verified by a simulation or a test.

Why Continuum tracks requirements alongside the design

Every simulation result in Module 5 — trajectories, budgets, sizing outputs — exists to answer one question: does this design satisfy its requirements? Requirements are the reference point that turns raw simulation numbers ("the battery holds 40 Wh") into a verdict ("...which is enough / not enough to survive the eclipse").

Analogy: requirements are like a grading rubric handed out before an exam. Without it, you can still take the exam (run the simulation) — but you have no way to know if you passed.
Module 3 · Spacecraft & Payload

Mass Budget

A spreadsheet-style accounting of exactly how many kilograms every part of the spacecraft weighs — and why every gram is fought over.

A rocket can only lift a fixed amount of mass to a given orbit. The mass budget lists every subsystem's weight, sums them up, and compares the total against that hard limit — with margin set aside for the inevitable surprises during design.

SubsystemTypical share of mass
StructureFrame and mechanical housing
Power (batteries + solar arrays)Often one of the heaviest subsystems
Propulsion (fuel + thrusters)Only present if the mission needs maneuvering
PayloadThe instrument itself
MarginDeliberate reserve for design changes and uncertainty
Why "margin" is its own line item: early in a mission, almost every subsystem's mass estimate is optimistic. Engineers intentionally reserve extra mass (often 20–30%) as a buffer, so that normal design refinement doesn't blow past the rocket's lift capacity later on.
Module 3 · Spacecraft & Payload

Power Budget

Every electrical device on the spacecraft draws power — and for roughly a third of every orbit, there's no sunlight to make more of it (see Eclipses).

Power generated over one orbit — zero during eclipse.

Example split of where consumed power goes.

The power budget balances two sides of a ledger: generation (solar arrays, sized by area and efficiency) against consumption (every subsystem and the payload, added together, including instruments that only run part of the time). Batteries bridge the gap during eclipse — they must be large enough to power essential systems through the longest expected shadow period without fully draining.

Why this budget is so tight: unlike mass (which is fixed once launched), power is a continuous requirement — the spacecraft must never run out, every single orbit, for its entire lifetime. A power budget that "just barely" balances on paper often fails once real-world losses (aging batteries, degraded solar cells, sensor pointing errors) are considered — which is why engineers build in margin here too.
Module 3 · Spacecraft & Payload

Mission Settings & Additional Force Models

Beyond gravity and drag, a handful of smaller forces can matter for specific missions.

Mission settings configure how the simulation should run — how long to simulate, how finely to step through time, and which of the forces below to include. Not every mission needs every force modeled; including more forces increases realism but also computation time, so teams turn on only what's relevant to their mission's precision needs.

☀️ Solar radiation pressure — sunlight itself carries a tiny amount of momentum; over months or years this can noticeably nudge a spacecraft's orbit, especially for missions needing pinpoint accuracy.
🌊 Third-body gravity — the Sun and Moon's gravity also tug on a spacecraft, not just the central body's — usually a small effect, but not always negligible.
🧲 Magnetic/other perturbations — additional minor forces sometimes modeled for very precise or long-duration missions.
⏱️ Simulation settings — things like total mission duration and time-step size, which trade off accuracy against how long the simulation takes to run.
Why it's presented as a checklist: think of these like optional camera filters — each one adds a layer of realism (and processing time). A quick early-design check might use only gravity and drag; a final pre-launch verification might switch on everything.
Module 4 · Targets & Ground Assets

Discrete vs Regional Targets

A mission's "target" is simply what part of the Earth it cares about — and there are two fundamentally different ways to define one.

🎯 Discrete Targets

A specific list of individual points — exact latitude/longitude locations, added one row at a time (a city, an airport, a single facility).

🗺️ Regional Targets

An entire area — a custom-drawn shape, a whole country, or a whole continent — rather than a single point.

Target typeSub-optionsGood for
DiscreteAdd individual rows (one point each)Point-to-point comms, imaging specific facilities/cities
RegionalCustom shape · World · Predefined regionsBroad-area monitoring — agriculture, weather, disaster coverage across a whole zone
Why the distinction matters: a discrete target asks "does the satellite pass directly over this exact point?" — a much stricter, less frequent event than a regional target's question, "does the satellite pass over any part of this large area?" This directly affects how often a requirement like "revisit this target daily" is realistically achievable.
Module 4 · Targets & Ground Assets

Resolution: How Finely to Grid a Region

A regional target isn't analyzed as one giant blob — it's broken into a grid of small cells, and "resolution" controls how fine that grid is.

Coarse resolution

Few, large grid cells — fast to compute, but low geographic detail.

Fine resolution

Many small grid cells — much more precise, but heavier to simulate.

Longitude resolution and latitude resolution set the width and height of each grid cell, typically in degrees (e.g. 1° × 1°). Every cell is then evaluated separately when the simulation checks coverage — so a finer grid gives a much more accurate picture of exactly which parts of a region get seen, at the cost of many more calculations.

Analogy: it's exactly like image resolution on a screen. A coarse grid is a low-resolution photo — you get the general shape but miss detail. A fine grid is a high-resolution photo — sharp detail, but a bigger file to process.

Regions themselves can be defined as a custom hand-drawn shape, the entire world, or one of several predefined regions (continents, countries, or standard geographic zones) — giving flexibility between quick global estimates and precise, custom-shaped areas of interest.

Module 4 · Targets & Ground Assets

Ground Stations & Ground Assets

A satellite is useless if it can never talk to anyone on the ground — ground assets are the "phone lines" of a mission.

A ground asset (most commonly a ground station) is a fixed location on Earth with an antenna capable of communicating with the spacecraft — sending commands up, and receiving data down. Just like targets, ground assets are defined by their location, and the simulation calculates exactly when the spacecraft is in view of each one.

⬆️ Uplink — commands and instructions sent from the ground station to the spacecraft.
⬇️ Downlink — collected data (images, measurements) sent from the spacecraft back down to the ground station.

Because a spacecraft is only in range of a given ground station for a limited window each orbit, mission designers often need multiple ground stations spread around the globe to guarantee frequent enough contact — the same "line of sight" logic used for imaging targets in the previous pages applies here too, just for two-way communication instead of one-way observation.

Why it matters: even a perfectly designed orbit and spacecraft are worthless if there's no reliable way to get the collected data back to Earth. Ground asset planning is often just as critical to mission success as the orbit or the payload itself.
Module 5 · Reading Simulation Results

Trajectory & Orbital Element Plots

Once a mission is run, the simulation hands back exactly where the spacecraft went — and the numbers behind that path.

Spacecraft trajectory results are the raw output of the simulation: the spacecraft's exact position over time. These are typically shown in one of two standard reference frames:

J2000 — a fixed, non-rotating frame pinned to the stars, useful for comparing orbits without Earth's rotation getting in the way.
IAU (body-fixed) — a frame that rotates with the central body, useful for seeing where the spacecraft is relative to actual geography (this is what produces ground tracks — see Module 2).

Example "orbital elements plot": altitude oscillating between perigee and apogee, orbit after orbit — a common way to visually confirm the orbit is stable and behaving as designed.

Why plot elements instead of raw coordinates? A raw (x, y, z) position over time is hard to interpret at a glance. Plotting the orbital elements themselves (Module 2) — like altitude or eccentricity over time — immediately reveals whether an orbit is stable, slowly decaying, or drifting in an unexpected way.
Module 5 · Reading Simulation Results

Spacecraft Timeline

A single chart answering "what is the spacecraft doing, and when?" across the whole mission.

Example timeline: overlapping bars show when the spacecraft is in eclipse, in view of a target, and in view of a ground station — all on the same time axis.

The spacecraft timeline lays out every recurring event side-by-side on one shared clock: eclipse windows (Module 2), target visibility windows (Module 4), and ground station contact windows (Module 4). Seeing them together answers practical questions a single chart of any one event can't:

🔎 Does a target pass ever overlap with a ground station pass? If so, data could potentially be downlinked immediately after collection.
🔋 Does the payload need to operate during eclipse? If a target window falls during a power-scarce eclipse period, that's a conflict worth catching early.
Why it matters: individually, an eclipse chart, a target-visibility chart, and a ground-station chart each look fine in isolation. It's only by lining them up on the same timeline that scheduling conflicts and opportunities become visible — this is often how mission designers catch problems before they become costly surprises.
Module 5 · Reading Simulation Results

Mass & Power Budget Outputs

This is where the budgets from Module 3 come back — not as plans, but as verified (or failed) results.

Module 3 introduced mass and power budgets as design plans — estimates made before running anything. The results here are the simulation's verdict on those plans: did the actual, simulated behavior confirm the spacecraft has enough mass margin and enough power to survive its worst-case orbit conditions?

Example comparison: planned budget vs. simulated worst-case need, for mass and power. A margin that turns negative means the design doesn't yet close.

✅ Budget closes: the design has enough margin — mass is under the launch limit, power generation exceeds worst-case demand even through the longest eclipse.
⚠️ Budget doesn't close: the simulation reveals a shortfall — time to reduce payload mass, add solar array area, change the orbit, or revisit the vendor selection (Module 3).
Why this loop matters: mission design is rarely a straight line. A failed budget output often sends the team back to Module 3 (spacecraft), Module 2 (orbit), or Module 4 (targets) to adjust the design, then re-run the mission — repeating until every budget closes with acceptable margin.
Module 5 · Reading Simulation Results

Sizing Outputs

The final "spec sheet" — the concrete numbers a real hardware team would need to actually build the spacecraft.

Sizing outputs translate all the earlier analysis (orbit, budgets, requirements) into concrete hardware specifications: how big does each component actually need to be? This is often the last stop before a mission design is considered "closed" and ready for more detailed engineering.

Sizing outputAnswers
Solar array areaHow many square meters of solar cells are needed to meet the power budget?
Battery capacityHow many watt-hours of storage are needed to survive the longest eclipse?
Propellant massHow much fuel is needed for the mission's planned lifetime, including drag makeup?
Antenna sizeHow large must the antenna be to close the communication link at the required data rate?
Why sizing outputs are the natural end point: everything in this portal — orbits, gravity models, targets, budgets — ultimately exists to answer one practical question: exactly what hardware does this mission need? Sizing outputs are that answer, expressed in units a manufacturer can actually build to.

Business Manager: The Budget Owner

Owns the purchase decision for the space-industry track. Not deeply technical themselves — their job is translating someone else's engineering into a number a budget can approve.

Their world

The Business Manager works at a space-industry company building out a real program (the documented real example: Thales's IMAGIN-e program). They're evaluating whether Sophia's orbital compute is worth the money for that specific program — not shopping in the abstract. They sit in the middle of an internal chain: they need to convince people above them, using numbers handed to them from below.

Likely already working with ◌ reasoned guess

📊 Budget/CapEx-OpEx models — standard corporate financial planning spreadsheets and business-case templates, used well before Sophia enters the picture.
📋 Program risk registers — whatever internal system tracks program-level risk, since "program risk" is explicitly part of the case they need to build.

Not documented in the source material — inferred from the fact that they think in "CapEx vs. OpEx" and "program risk," which are standard corporate finance concepts, not space-engineering ones.

Relationship to other stakeholders

➡️ Forwards claims to their Technical Expert — the documented default pattern: BM configures, then hands off technical claims for validation.
🔁 Sometimes it's one joint call, not a handoff ✓ confirmed in a real deal — Julian (BM, owns IMAGIN-e) and Alejandro (their Technical Expert) were on the same call making a joint decision.

Best-fit use case: Budget/Risk framing vs. technical validation

The same underlying configuration gets read two completely different ways. The Business Manager needs the budget/risk framing half — dollar terms, CapEx/OpEx, program risk — because that's the only language that lets them "hand it upward without a meeting."

Ideal outcome: a Trade Study PDF credible enough to socialize internally and convert into a signed deal — not a technical document they'd have to translate themselves.

What goes wrong if we get it wrong

A configuration that's technically excellent but doesn't translate into a defensible dollar figure is useless to this persona — they can't act on engineering rigor they can't put in front of a budget committee.

✓ confirmed in a real deal: trust survives an honest, corrected pricing mistake — the correction itself became a trust-building moment, not a deal-breaker. Getting a number wrong isn't fatal; hiding it would be.
Module 6 · Stakeholder Deep Dive

Technical Expert: The Sign-Off

No purchase authority at all — but the only persona explicitly paired with another, because no deal closes without their signature on the technical annex.

Their world

The Technical Expert's entire job is to make sure their company doesn't sign a contract based on false numbers. They work directly with the Business Manager, auditing every technical claim and re-running the BLADE/latency numbers against whatever scenario the Business Manager has chosen.

✓ confirmed in a real deal: this role isn't always in-house staff — it can be an external contracted validator, like a research partner the buyer brings in specifically to check the numbers.

Likely already working with ◌ reasoned guess

🛰️ Orbital/compute analysis tools — the source material mentions "their own measured TOPS" and "their own ground-contact schedule," implying they already run some form of orbital-mechanics or chip-benchmarking toolchain before Mission Planner ever enters the picture.
📎 Vendor data sheets — cross-checking stats against data sheets is documented directly; that implies a habit of keeping/comparing spec sheets from multiple hardware vendors.

This is exactly why Modules 2 and 3 of this portal exist: gravity models, drag, mass and power budgets are the Technical Expert's native language. A Business Manager reads a Trade Study PDF; a Technical Expert reads the assumptions behind it.

Relationship to other stakeholders

Paired tightly with the Business Manager — receives forwarded claims, must validate and sign before the deal can close. Not connected to Procurement Officer, DC Operator, or Press & Influencer at all.

Best-fit use case: Budget/Risk framing vs. technical validation

Same configuration as the Business Manager's use case, opposite half: the Technical Expert needs the technical validation side — source-cited, substitutable models they can independently re-derive, not just a number to trust.

What goes wrong if we get it wrong

If a claim isn't source-cited or the underlying model isn't substitutable with the Technical Expert's own numbers, they can't sign — and the deal simply stalls at exactly the step it can't survive.

Ideal outcome: they sign off confident every figure is defensible, and start Onboarding (Phase III) already fluent in the toolchain instead of learning it from scratch.

Procurement Officer: The Independent Auditor

A government buyer running a completely separate evaluation track — explicitly called the "highest-value" audience in the source documentation.

Their world

The Procurement Officer wants Sophia's onboard AI computer to fly on satellites for a military mission — detecting missile launches or tracking objects in space for national security. Unlike the Business Manager, they don't have flexibility on process: strict government rules apply, and every document has to survive an audit, not just convince a person.

Confirmed already in use

🏛️ DoD Architecture Framework (DoDAF) & JCIDS — named directly in the source material as the institutional-legitimacy frameworks this persona expects to see referenced.
📄 Source Selection / pre-RFP process — the ideal outcome is explicitly a document "attachable to a pre-RFP technical assessment," meaning they're already operating inside a formal federal acquisition process.

Relationship to other stakeholders

Fully independent track — no documented handoff to or from the Business Manager, Technical Expert, DC Operator, or Press & Influencer. Their evaluation stands alone.

Best-fit use cases: Hypersonic/SDA Modeling & SDA Sensor Fusion

Both are defense scenarios modeled independently at constellation scale, built to read as neutral engineering reports — not sales collateral. That distinction is the whole point for this persona.

What they need, concretely ✓ confirmed in a real deal: institutional-legitimacy cues (JPL/Microsoft pedigree) and survivable rigor, plus — before signing — a refund policy, a 90-day minimum commitment structure, clear IP/distribution rights, and a data-isolation guarantee.

What goes wrong if we get it wrong

A document that reads as polished sales material instead of a neutral engineering report won't survive their stress test — and one that can't attach to a pre-RFP assessment without rework defeats the entire point of using Mission Planner here.

✓ confirmed in a real deal — cross-cutting risk: the data-isolation gap (no hypervisor between co-tenants) is a client-admitted risk that touches this persona directly, alongside the Business Manager and Technical Expert tracks.

DC Operator: The Capacity Planner

Not building a space mission at all — just asking whether renting a bit of orbital compute beats building another terrestrial data center.

Their world

The DC Operator already runs large terrestrial data centers. Sophia isn't a space product to them — it's a capacity and cost question, evaluated the same way they'd evaluate any other fleet expansion decision. They model orbital capacity as a federated, burstable extension of infrastructure they already operate.

Likely already working with ◌ reasoned guess

🗄️ DCIM / fleet capacity tools — data center infrastructure management systems, to track utilization across their existing terrestrial fleet.
🌱 Sustainability financing frameworks — the ideal outcome explicitly mentions a "sustainability-linked financing decision," implying they already operate inside an ESG/financing process.

Relationship to other stakeholders

Fully independent track, same as Procurement Officer — no routing through the Business Manager or Technical Expert. No new evidence from the Thales deal touches this persona yet.

Best-fit use case: Non-hyperscale AI inference burst + backup/DR

Sized to a 5–50kW tier and framed explicitly as a federated extension of an existing fleet — matching exactly how this persona already thinks about capacity, in kilowatts, not orbital mechanics.

What they need: a concrete kW-to-BLADE conversion, an architectural answer to maintenance risk (who fixes what, and how, once it's in orbit), and a sustainability-financing angle their board will recognize.

What goes wrong if we get it wrong

This persona's entire mental model is kilowatts, racks, and uptime. If Mission Planner can't translate "BLADE" into their existing capacity language clearly and immediately, the pitch never lands — no matter how good the underlying economics are.

Ideal outcome: a board-ready Orbital Compute Audit that supports a sustainability-linked financing decision — a document for their board, not for an engineer.

Press & Influencer: Outside Every Deal

Not a buyer at all. The only persona whose entire relationship to Mission Planner is a single, frictionless interaction.

Their world

Arrives cold — from a press release or a newsletter, not from a sales conversation. Isn't evaluating anything or trying to justify a purchase to anyone. Wants exactly one cool fact or chart for an article or post, and wants it immediately.

Likely already working with ◌ reasoned guess

Whatever they use to publish — a CMS, a social platform, a newsletter tool — plus whatever press kit or release brought them in. None of this is documented in the source material; it's a reasonable assumption based on how this persona is described, not a cited fact.

One-directional and outside the deal entirely: this persona seeds Awareness for the Business Manager's track specifically, but nothing flows back to them. No handoff, no follow-up loop.
